Can a real fly brain learn to imagine the world like a video AI?

A short visual story of the experiment, what we found, and what did not work. Read the five scenes first. The numbers come after.

SCENE 1 OF 5

A camera walks around a world.

Two worlds. A simple test arena with coloured pillars, and a digital copy of Times Square, New York, built from the city's real building records.

The camera can only do five things: step forward, step back, turn left, turn right, stay. Each walk lasts 24 steps.

SCENE 2 OF 5

A big AI describes each moment.

The teacher is a large video AI (V-JEPA 2.1). For every step of the walk it writes down a fingerprint of the scene: a list of 256 numbers that captures what is where.

We never change the teacher. It only provides the answers.

SCENE 3 OF 5

A fly brain tries to write the same fingerprint.

The student is built from 6,935 real neurons of a fruit fly, connected exactly as in the fly's brain (from the FlyWire wiring map).

It gets much less than the teacher: a tiny 32 × 8 pixel picture, plus the last move it made. From that it must guess the teacher's 256 numbers.

Training means: compare the guess with the teacher's fingerprint, nudge the brain's connection strengths, repeat. The wiring pattern itself never changes.

All the brain gets to see, 32 × 8 pixels:

SCENE 4 OF 5

Then the lights go off.

For 8 steps in the middle of every walk the student gets no picture at all. It only knows its own moves: "turned left", "stepped forward".

It still has to guess the teacher's fingerprint. So it must imagine how the scene moves as it moves.

A real fly does exactly this: it keeps track of which way it is facing in the dark, from its own turns.

SCENE 5 OF 5

Does the real wiring matter?

This is the actual question. We train the same neurons five times with different wiring:

🧠 Real fly wiring
🔀 Scrambled: same neurons, same number of links each, partners shuffled
🎲 Random: same number of links, placed anywhere
🕸️ Everything connected
📦 A standard AI memory unit (GRU) with no biology at all

If the real wiring guesses best, the fly's wiring diagram is a genuinely useful starting point for imagining the world.

What we found

One score decides it: how wrong the guess is in the dark. Shorter bar = better. A bar at 1.0 would mean "no better than always guessing the average".

✓
The real wiring beats scrambled, random and fully-connected, in both worlds. Same neurons, same number of links. Only the real pattern wins. So the fly's wiring diagram is a useful head start, not just a sparsity trick.
✓
The real-wired brain keeps a sense of direction in the dark. From its neurons alone we can read out which way it faces to within about 10° in the arena. The fully-connected brain and the GRU lose it (36° to 45° error). Nobody told it about heading.
✗
The standard AI unit (GRU) still guesses better in the test arena. So we cannot claim the fly wiring beats everything. In Times Square the fly wiring did beat it, but that is one run on a small dataset.
✗
Using the fly brain to plan a route does not work yet. When it imagines moves and picks the best, it reaches the goal only 3 to 7 % of the time. A random walker gets 7 %. The planner needs to be rebuilt.
?
Open: the fly's own compass cells (51 "EPG" neurons) do not carry the direction signal; the rest of the brain does. And joining two or three brain copies together gave no gain.

For the technical reader

Exact setup

Teacher: V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-B at 256 px, frozen. Per-step latent (3,840 dims) projected by PCA to 256 (94.6 % variance on the arena, 91 % on NYC).

Student: leaky recurrent network h ← (1−a)h + a·tanh(W h + W_in·obs·visible + W_act·action + b), a = 0.5, N = 6,935 FlyWire v783 neurons from four regions (VIS 2,330, CX 2,875, MB 427, OUT 1,303), 91,663 synapse pairs. Non-zero weights only on real pairs; sign fixed by presynaptic neurotransmitter (Dale's law) via softplus. Visual input enters VIS neurons only; actions enter CX and OUT. Readout: linear from all neurons to 256.

Training: 20 epochs (arena) or 30 (NYC), AdamW lr 2e-3, batch 32, random blind windows of 8 steps with 3× loss weight on blind steps, cosine schedule. Evaluation: 8 visible steps then 8 blind steps on held-out episodes.

Controls: degree-preserving shuffle (postsynaptic partners permuted), Erdős–Rényi random with equal edge count and sign fractions, dense (55 M params), GRU with 1,400 hidden units (9.5 M params, parameter-matched).

Worlds: synthetic arena (12 m square, 4 landmark pillars, 360° panorama, tasks: navigate to a landmark, push a box to a zone), 1,000 train / 200 test episodes. NYC: Valdrada digital twin around Times Square, 110° forward camera, 120 train / 30 test episodes, step 4 m, goals 30–60 m away along sidewalks.

What to do next
  1. Rebuild the planner (goal encoding and search) before any planning comparison.
  2. Store the recurrent weights sparsely so three or more brain copies fit in memory and checkpoints shrink from 420 MB.
  3. More NYC data and more seeds; collection runs at about 80 s per walk on a laptop, so this needs a GPU machine.
  4. Close or explain the GRU gap: longer training, learnable time constants, or reframe the claim around the sparse controls and the heading code.
Words used on this page

Fingerprint / latent: a list of numbers a model uses to summarise what it sees.
Connectome / wiring map: the complete list of which neurons connect to which, measured from a real brain.
FlyWire: the project that mapped every neuron and synapse of an adult fruit-fly brain.
In the dark / blind window: the 8 steps where the student gets no picture.
Sense of direction / heading: which way the camera is pointing. We read it out of the neurons with a simple linear fit.
GRU: a standard building block of AI sequence models, with no biological structure.
